# Fix the counting bug, then exploit selectively

The single biggest weakness in our Short Deck PLO5 bot is not strategic. It is a **one-line statistics bug in `bot/opponent.py`**: opponent folds end the hand before our next decision, so they are never counted, and the opponent's preflop raise frequency is inflated. Against jammer the estimate converges to **about 0.99 when the true rate is 0.30**. As a result the range model barely narrows, the bot overestimates its equity against jams by **about 4.5 points**, and it continues against **98.4% of jams when roughly half should be folds**. Counting those unseen folds ("fixcount") moves the jammer result from **-1.38 to +0.37 bb/deal** and mc_grinder from **+1.01 (3-7-0) to +1.39 (10-0-0)**, with no regressions across 15 other opponents. The challenger demon.py ties agent.py head to head. Its whole field advantage comes from two cheap preflop features, sd_use and defend, that partly work around the same bug. Its expert switching, Q tables and online learning add nothing or do harm. So the fastest path to more tournament points has three steps. First, fix the opponent statistics. Second, port sd_use and re-test defend on top of that fix. Third, add a stack-off rule and an opponent-response model to the one-step EV formula. Only after those land should we add a small, frequency-based classifier that switches into targeted modes, such as anti-jammer folding and limping, a stack-off mode at low SPR, and possibly a gated aggression mode. Chip-reward bandits, online Q-learning and a whole-game jamming strategy all fail on the evidence. Almost every gain below was measured separately against the current buggy agent.py, and several of them repair the same range error. Their sum overstates the combined gain, and the stacked configuration has not yet been tested.

## One counting bug drives most of the jammer loss

Jammer is a trivially exploitable opponent. It pot-raises the top 30% of hands at every chance, folds everything else to any bet, and never folds once it has raised ([jammer_leaks](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/jammer_leaks.md>); [dev/sparring/jammer.py](../dev/sparring/jammer.py)). The best response is to steal freely and to continue against its jams only when equity against its real range clears the all-in price. At 80-chip stacks, every continue against jammer is a stack-off, so the required equity is (80 − chips already in) / 160. That is **48.75% for the big blind facing an open** and **46.25% for the button facing a re-raise**. Our bot does roughly the opposite. It folded only **0.6% of hands preflop**, re-raised 74% of the time, and won only **43.3% of showdowns** against jammer's 53.3%. Showdowns account for **-12.7 bb/deal lost against +10.3 won**, while the folds jammer hands us are worth only +1.04 ([leaks_jammer.txt](/private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-armaanmediratta-Desktop-datathon-heads-up-bundle/2424e626-b048-4c5c-b38d-bbae1d4a40ae/scratchpad/leaks_jammer.txt)). The big blind carries most of the loss (-1.13 of -1.46 bb/deal). The biggest losing hands are 40 bb preflop raising wars with marginal holdings.

The mechanism is now fully traced. `observe()` counts opponent actions only when it is our turn ([bot/opponent.py:45-64](../bot/opponent.py)). The `HandResult` passed to `on_hand_end` contains no action history ([game.py:273-287](../game.py)). So when jammer folds as button, or folds in the big blind to our raise, the fold is never recorded. The estimated preflop raise rate becomes "raises per non-fold action", and the range cut-off `1 − r_pf^n` collapses to **about 0.014**. The measured weighting shift was only -0.011 equity, so the bot effectively prices every jam as a random hand ([bot/policy.py:52](../bot/policy.py)). Calibration over 2,322 jam-facing decisions confirms this. The ordering of hands is fine, but the level is wrong: the estimated mean equity is 0.491 against a true 0.450, consistently in every equity bin ([jammer_leaks](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/jammer_leaks.md>)). The same unnarrowed range also leads the raise EV to assume **about 45% fold equity when re-raising an opponent that never folds**.

A second, opposite bias sits in `fold_to_raise`. A call that ends the hand, such as a river call or a call of an all-in, is never counted, but folds always are. So the bot overestimates how often opponents fold to its raises: **0.411 against a true 0.347 vs jammer, and 0.267 against 0.239 vs exploiter** ([policy_audit](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/policy_audit.md>)). Both counters are also pooled across very different situations. `pf_raise_freq` mixes opens with re-raises, and `fold_to_raise` mixes steals with 4-bets. Against jammer, the measured 0.446 blends a 70% fold rate against our opens with a 0% fold rate once it has raised. None of this is jammer-specific. Every fold-heavy or tight opponent has its raise frequency inflated, which is why fixcount also improved **nit from +1.02 to +1.77** ([regress.txt](/private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-armaanmediratta-Desktop-datathon-heads-up-bundle/2424e626-b048-4c5c-b38d-bbae1d4a40ae/scratchpad/regress.txt)).

Parameter tuning cannot fix this. Eight PARAMS overrides all stayed within about ±0.3 bb/deal of the jammer baseline, and every opponent-specific bundle in the switching grid still lost 1.7-2.1 bb/deal to jammer. The range parameters never bind when the estimated rate is 0.99 ([strategy_switching_experiments](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/strategy_switching_experiments.md>)).

## The one-step EV formula has six structural blind spots

Apart from the counting bugs, the decision model in `bot/policy.py` has flaws that fixcount does not touch. The audit ranks them by EV at stake. Most were found by worked examples and instrumented logs, not by harness A/B tests, so their gains are estimates ([policy_audit](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/policy_audit.md>)).

**The opponent can never re-raise.** In the model, a raise wins `fa·pot` or gets called. A re-raise is never modelled, even though the game allows four raises per street. With the bot's real memory against jammer, the formula rates a 3-bet at **+0.8 to +1.3 chips when its true value is -2.4 to -15 chips**. Against a test opponent that 3-bets 15%, it overstates every open by **about 0.35-0.4 bb**, and with its weakest hands it picks a losing raise over a profitable limp. The fix is a small response tree (fold, call or re-raise, then our fold or call) built from per-situation fold and re-raise statistics. It reuses the samples already drawn and costs **under 0.3 ms**. The audit calls it the highest-value structural change, at 1-2 days of work. A 2-4 hour partial version splits `fold_to_raise` by situation and adds a single penalty for being re-raised.

**Ranges ignore draws and compound too fast.** `opp_now` ranks opponent hands by their current made hand only. Its rank correlation with true equity was 0.38-0.71 on test flops, which overstates our equity by **2-11 points** against same-width ranges. Earlier-street bets are judged on the current board, which drops missed-draw bluffs from river ranges. And `1 − r^n` treats a repeat 30% raiser as top-10%. Averaging the opponent's percentile over four extra runouts improves the ranking on wet boards (0.57 vs 0.38) for about 0.1-0.2 ms. A visible symptom is overfolding: the bot folds **58%, 66% and 72%** to single bets on the flop, turn and river against exploiter, while minimum defence against a pot bet is 50%. Against demon, folding the flop to a pot bet (53% of the time) cost **-0.97 bb/deal** ([leaks_agent_vs_demon.txt](/private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-armaanmediratta-Desktop-datathon-heads-up-bundle/2424e626-b048-4c5c-b38d-bbae1d4a40ae/scratchpad/avd/leaks_agent_vs_demon.txt)).

**Realization is a constant, and calls never commit.** R is 0.95 in position and 0.85 out of position. Raises switch to R = 1 when committed, but calls never do, so the bot is biased toward jamming over calling at low SPR. Facing a 4-bet it jammed 228 times, folded 72 and called 0. The commit threshold also misses the single-raised-pot structure: a flop bet plus a pot raise puts 60 of 74 chips in, yet the bot does not treat that as committed. The fix is a one-line commitment test on calls, or a per-sample showdown-pot model that captures implied and reverse-implied odds for about 0.05 ms.

**Preflop is nearly always-raise.** The bot opens 99.7% from the small blind, raises 99.7% against limps, and raises 99.8% of button hands against jammer. Back-of-envelope arithmetic, not yet tested, says that against jammer limping weak hands earns **about +0.45 chips per hand** where raising and then folding to the 3-bet loses -0.4 ([jammer_leaks](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/jammer_leaks.md>)). That gap is the most plausible route from a jammer chop to a jammer win. A precomputed table of equity against the top-x% ranges (about 3 MB, about 25 minutes to build offline) would remove preflop Monte Carlo noise. It would also free 0.5-3.4 ms per preflop decision and give the response tree exact equities for each part of the opponent's range.

**Play is deterministic and capped.** The final action is a plain argmax, and **0% of flop and river checks held equity above 0.6**. A bot that bets whenever we check would profit. No field bot does this yet, so the fix (trap 15-25% of strong out-of-position hands, randomize among near-tied actions, guard against our own overfolding) is insurance rather than measured gain.

**Monte Carlo noise flips close decisions while time sits unused.** Range weighting cuts the effective sample size to 16-50% of the samples drawn. As a result, **25% of flop spots facing a bet flip their decision across reruns even at 1,536 samples**. Exact river enumeration costs about 1 ms plus a cheaper ranking step. Adaptive extra sampling, used only when the gap between the top two actions is under 2 standard errors in pots of 10 bb or more, would target the spots where noise costs chips.

The legality audit found no action-legality bugs: the fold/check handling, the raise clamps and `safety.legalize` are all correct. The risk on that side is timing, not illegal moves.

## Demon's edge is two preflop features, not its learning machinery

Head to head, agent.py and demon.py are a **statistical tie in every run**, with point estimates from -0.14 to +0.30 bb/deal and every CI spanning zero. Demon scored 43/48 field points against agent's 41/48. That gap comes almost entirely from jammer (paired Δ **+1.25 [+0.99, +1.52]**), flush_hunter (+0.99 to +1.28), nit (+0.56) and baseline_always_pot (+1.03) ([agent_vs_demon](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/agent_vs_demon.md>)). Turning off demon's two preflop additions makes it identical to agent (+0.015 vs jammer). The two features are **sd_use**, which blends the frequency cut with the 20th percentile of hands the opponent actually showed down after n raises, and **defend**, which adds `0.15·pot·max(0, r_pf − 0.35)` to preflop call EV. Ported into agent, sd_use alone gives **+0.63** vs jammer, defend alone +0.22, and both together **+1.34** (superadditive). Across 14 opponents both together average **about +0.44 bb/deal**, with gains on always_half (+1.74), flush_hunter (+0.91) and nit (+0.58). The cost is a small but significant loss on trapper (-0.13) and a non-significant trend on tag (-0.28) ([results_paired.txt](/private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-armaanmediratta-Desktop-datathon-heads-up-bundle/2424e626-b048-4c5c-b38d-bbae1d4a40ae/scratchpad/avd/results_paired.txt); [results3.txt](/private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-armaanmediratta-Desktop-datathon-heads-up-bundle/2424e626-b048-4c5c-b38d-bbae1d4a40ae/scratchpad/avd/results3.txt)).

Both features interact with the counting bug, and this matters for planning. sd_use is a data-driven workaround: it replaces the bogus frequency-based floor with the floor the opponent actually shows down. It should still add information once fixcount lands. defend is perverse. It fires harder the more `r_pf` is overestimated, so its threshold was tuned against biased inputs, and it may become neutral or harmful once `r_pf` is correct. Demon inherits the same fold-count bug, which is its own biggest weakness.

The rest of demon's design should be dropped, not ported. The meta selector is greedy over an all-zero prior with no exploration, so any expert that goes negative in a cell loses to an untried expert at zero. It switched experts **18-87 times per 1,000-hand half**, against the docstring's claim that it plays grinder ([strategy_switching_experiments](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/strategy_switching_experiments.md>)). Pinned experts are all within ±0.33 bb/deal of agent, with CIs spanning zero. Default demon minus pinned grinder averages about zero with mixed signs. The Q-lens tables are untrained, since `demon_q.npz` holds only expert parameters, and a demon configured like agent matches agent exactly (+0.000 [-0.109, +0.110]). The CEM-tuned grinder parameters add nothing inside agent. **DEMON_ONLINE=1 significantly hurts (-0.58 [-1.11, -0.05])**. Demon's real engineering win is economy. It spends 1.88 s per half against agent's 3.94 s (agent peaks at **5.66 s of the 8 s bank** against always_pot) with no measurable accuracy cost. agent capped at 512 samples was indistinguishable from the default, but demon at 256 samples lost **-0.51 [-0.96, -0.06]** to mc_grinder ([agent_vs_demon](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/agent_vs_demon.md>)). The two notes agree once reconciled. The uniform 1,536-sample cap wastes time on easy spots, and the bank should be reallocated to rivers and close, high-pot decisions rather than spent uniformly. It should not simply be left unused.

## Switch strategies on action frequencies, never on chip results

The opponent statistics identify bots quickly. Nearest-centroid classification on the three opponent rates plus postflop actions per hand recognised the 16 zoo bots with **80% accuracy at hand 25, 89% at hand 50 and 98% at hand 100** ([classify.py](/private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-armaanmediratta-Desktop-datathon-heads-up-bundle/2424e626-b048-4c5c-b38d-bbae1d4a40ae/scratchpad/ssw/classify.py)). The clusters that need different handling are clear by hand 50: preflop jammer, solid grinder, nit, station. This matches binomial arithmetic. Preflop frequencies get one opportunity per hand and settle to ±0.10 within about 100 hands, while chip results with σ ≈ 28 bb/hand carry a ±1.7 bb/hand CI even over a full half ([opponent_modelling_literature](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/opponent_modelling_literature.md>)). The prize from switching is modest, though. The best of six parameter bundles beat the default on fresh seeds only for **aggr vs mc_grinder (+0.36 [+0.005, +0.72]) and passive vs jammer (+0.29 [+0.09, +0.48])**. A third screen winner, rlo vs nit, did not replicate. The best bundle also depends on the opponent: aggr helps against mc_grinder but costs **-0.34 against nit**. The realistic oracle upside is **+1 to +1.5 W-C-L points per 16 opponents** ([strategy_switching_experiments](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/strategy_switching_experiments.md>)). All of it was measured under the buggy statistics, so the classifier's features and the bundle-to-opponent mapping will both shift once fixcount lands.

Reward-driven selection fails on noise. To separate arms whose gap is 0.25 bb/hand at σ = 15 takes **about 27,700 hands per arm**. A Thompson-sampling simulation over seven arms picked the best arm at roughly chance rate (14-20%) and recovered only **0-25% of the oracle gain** within 1,000 hands. The literature agrees. The ACPC's best adaptive agent built a 4-response portfolio offline and chose online with Exp4, playing a *mixture* of the responses with a 2% floor so every arm could be evaluated off-policy with importance sampling. It won 732.5 mbb/h against 368.7 for equilibrium, and **UCB over single arms did worse with double the variance** ([Bard et al., AAMAS 2013](https://poker.cs.ualberta.ca/publications/AAMAS13-modelling.pdf)). Variance reduction such as AIVAT cut the standard deviation by 85% in no-limit hold'em ([Burch et al., AAAI 2018](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/11481)). Even halving our variance leaves the required sample an order of magnitude above 1,000 hands. Online tabular Q-learning is worse still, because thousands of cells share the same noise, and demon's online mode measured negative. The design that survives is a rule-based classifier with 3-4 profiles. It plays default until about hand 75, switches only after the same profile is returned at 2-3 consecutive checks 25 hands apart, falls back to default when the nearest centroid is far away, and reverts if a profile's cumulative result falls about 3 standard errors below zero. That last rule is a guardrail, not a learner. Naive exploitation of a slightly wrong model can be catastrophic: in Flop Hold'em the payoff fell to -88.9 while exploitability rose from 29 to 981 ([Liu et al., NeurIPS 2022](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/b12a1d1014e952e676f5d6931d03241a-Paper-Conference.pdf)). So every mode must be restricted, gated and reversible.

## Jamming pays only as a targeted mode

The user's idea of a jamming strategy deserves a split verdict. **As a whole-game strategy it is weak against this field.** Our current bot beats the jam-heavy bots by wide margins: maniac by +10.48 bb/deal, baseline_always_pot comfortably, and jammer itself becomes a chop once our calling is fixed ([regress.txt](/private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-armaanmediratta-Desktop-datathon-heads-up-bundle/2424e626-b048-4c5c-b38d-bbae1d4a40ae/scratchpad/regress.txt); [strategy_switching_experiments](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/strategy_switching_experiments.md>)). Pot-limit also makes "jam" a sequence rather than an action. Preflop pot raises go to 6, 18, 54 and then 80, so committing takes 3-4 raises, and each step gives a competent opponent a pot-odds decision ([policy_audit](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/policy_audit.md>)). Preflop equities are compressed, too: the 5th and 95th percentile hands have 0.389 and 0.589 equity against a random hand. So raw strength cannot carry an all-in-every-hand plan, and any jammer is beaten by a bot that simply folds correctly.

Three targeted forms are worth testing, in descending order of evidence. **First, a stack-off (all-in-or-fold) mode at low SPR or against committed re-raisers.** It is already partly measured. Treating preflop calls and raises against an opponent who re-raises more than 60% of the time when continuing as all-in decisions moved jammer from -1.38 to +0.22 bb/deal without the count fix. On top of fixcount it added only +0.04 (not significant) but tightened the CI enough for a significant positive result. It may cost about -0.13 against mc_grinder ([jammer_leaks](<../research_notes/Bot weaknesses and improvement options/jammer_leaks.md>)). The general version is the call-branch commitment test from the EV audit: once SPR after the call is below about 0.6-1, price both the call and the raise as all-in, and choose between all-in and fold. **Second, an anti-jammer mode selected by the classifier** (preflop raise rate of at least 0.8 with few postflop actions). It folds below the all-in threshold, limps weak buttons instead of raising and folding to the re-raise, and steals cheaply. This is the only identified path to a jammer WIN, and it is untested. **Third, a jam or aggression mode against over-folders.** The evidence here is mixed. The aggr bundle won +0.36 against mc_grinder but **lost -0.34 against nit**, the most obvious over-folder, because the existing raise EV already harvests fold equity, and extra aggression mostly bloats pots against nit's strong continuing range. If it is tested at all, the gate should be the fold rate in a specific spot (for example fold-to-open above 0.6 *and* re-raise rate below 0.1), measured after the counting fixes, and it should be confirmed on fresh seeds.

## Conclusion

The headline lesson is that the opponent model failed before the strategy did. Most of what looked like strategic weakness against jammer, and most of demon's apparent superiority, traces back to statistics that never saw an opponent fold. That changes how to read every earlier experiment. Results measured on the buggy bot, including the per-opponent bundle mappings, defend's threshold and sd_use's size, are provisional until they are re-measured on fixed statistics. Fix-first sequencing is therefore a methodological necessity, not only an efficiency choice.

The second lesson concerns where the remaining points are. Against the current zoo, most matches are already wins. The points still in play sit in a few chop-or-loss matchups, mainly jammer, mc_grinder, demon and equity_caller, and the WIN bar of about 0.062 per-deal standard deviations is set by variance as much as by edge. That favours folding correctly, controlling pot size against strong ranges, and small, reversible exploit modes chosen from fast action frequencies. It does not favour bigger aggression, learned switching from chip results, or a jamming identity. Against unknown tournament bots, the robust core plus cheap, gated modes is also the safer bet.
